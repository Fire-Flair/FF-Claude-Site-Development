# How to build a bartender profile that gets booked

A good profile answers a booker's questions in seconds: who you are, what you do, where you work and whether people trust you. Lead with a clear photo, list your roles in the words clients search, show your work areas, and collect reviews after every event.

## The structure that works

1. **A clear photo.** Bright, friendly, ideally you at work. Keep your face and head fully in frame.
2. **Your professions.** Use the titles people type: cocktail bartender, flair bartender, mixologist, bar manager, barback. See [the difference between a bartender, mixologist and flair bartender](/stories/bartender-mixologist-flair-bartender-difference).
3. **Where you work.** Add the areas you cover. Your Profile Card appears by area, never by your address.
4. **Evidence of skill.** Name what you can make and run: a classic menu, high-volume service, event setup, solo bars. Be specific and be honest.
5. **Reviews.** Ask the person who booked you to leave one after the event. A few good reviews do more than any description.
6. **Your people.** Add colleagues you have worked with. A connection means you know each other; it does not prove you worked a particular event together.

## Example (fictional)

> **Sam R.** Cocktail bartender · Hertfordshire and north London
> Eight years in hotel and event bars. Classic cocktail menus, solo bar setup for up to 60 guests, alcohol-free menus. Travels with own tools. Can bring a barback by arrangement.

This is an invented example to show the level of detail. Do not copy it. Use your own facts.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Claiming experience you cannot back up.
- Using a photo that hides your face or is cropped badly.
- Listing every possible role. A focused profile is easier to book.

Your public profile also works alongside a CV. See [how to write a bartender CV](/stories/how-to-write-a-bartender-cv).
